South Sudan · 79 counties · 2018–2025

Climate Trends in South Sudan

How water, heat, land and air have changed across South Sudan since 2018, shown next to conflict, displacement, hunger and aid funding. Built from NASA and other satellite records and from UN and research datasets.

Executive summary

This dashboard brings together 18 climate and environment measures and 10 measures of conflict, food and aid for each of South Sudan’s 79 counties, month by month from January 2018 to December 2025. For each county and measure, the map shows the trend: the typical change per year since 2018, estimated with Sen’s slope, a method that is not thrown off by single extreme months. For monthly data, each month is compared with the same month of 2018, so the normal swing between dry and rainy seasons does not count as a trend. Where a test shows the trend is unlikely to be chance, the county is outlined.

The clearest signal is water. Since 2018, the land has been holding more water, flood water has spread further across the Sudd wetland, and floods and other disasters have displaced hundreds of thousands of people in most years. Over the same period, money for the UN aid appeal fell sharply in 2025, when US government funding dropped to less than half of its 2024 level.

Key findings

  1. The land is storing more water. NASA GRACE satellites, which weigh the water held in soils, wetlands and aquifers, show a rise in 76 of 79 counties (a statistically significant trend in each of them). The typical rise is 3.7 cm of water per year, and the yearly national average went from +1.4 cm in 2018 to +16.2 cm in 2025, compared with the 2002–2020 average.
  2. The Sudd wetland is staying flooded. In the 16 counties of the Sudd, the average area under water each month grew from about 2,800 km² in 2018 to 11,000–14,000 km² in 2021–2025. The share of each county under water rose by a typical 1.3 percentage points per year, with a significant rise in 12 of the 16 counties.
  3. Rain over South Sudan itself shows no clear trend. NASA’s IMERG rainfall record shows no significant change in any county. The extra water on the land is therefore not explained by rain falling inside the country alone.
  4. Floods are a major cause of displacement. The International Organization for Migration (IOM) recorded about 475,000 people displaced by floods in each of 2020 and 2021, 416,000 in 2022 and 360,000 in 2024. In 2023, about 396,000 people arrived fleeing the war in Sudan.
  5. The ground got cooler by day, while the air got slightly warmer. MODIS satellites show daytime ground temperatures falling, with a significant drop in 63 counties (typically −0.34 °C per year). Air temperature rose slightly, by about +0.02 °C per year nationally, but not significantly in any single county.
  6. The air got hazier. Haze from dust and smoke (aerosol optical depth) rose in 75 of 79 counties, with a significant trend in each of them, by about 2% per year. Active fires show no clear national trend.
  7. Violence against civilians stayed high. The Armed Conflict Location & Event Data project (ACLED) recorded 563 civilians killed in attacks in 2018 and 1,670–1,907 a year in 2021–2023. In 2025, IOM recorded about 337,000 people displaced by national-level conflict, up from about 7,000 in 2024.
  8. Hunger is persistent. Averaged across counties, 44–54% of people faced crisis-level hunger or worse (IPC Phase 3+) in every year with data.
  9. Aid fell sharply in 2025. The UN aid appeal received 46% of the money requested in 2025 (US$787 million of US$1.70 billion), against 56–76% in 2018–2024. US government humanitarian funding fell from US$708 million in 2024 to US$282 million in 2025, and the US share of all humanitarian funding fell from 50% to 31%. The number of aid organisations reporting work in each county also declined over the period.

Reading the trends with care. Eight years is a short record. A trend describes 2018–2025 and should not be extended into the future. Some sources start later than 2018 or have gaps (for example, MODIS has no data for July–October 2025, and IOM Event Tracking starts in 2020). Counts of events depend on what gets reported. Each measure’s caveats are shown when you select it on the map.
