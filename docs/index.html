Climate Trends in South Sudan

Tracing changes in climate variables across South Sudan since 2018 and understanding their connection to conflict, displacement, food insecurity, and USAID funding flows.

Sources: NASA, Copernicus, United Nations

Executive summary

This dashboard brings together 17 climate and environment measures and 10 measures of conflict, food and aid for each of South Sudan's 79 counties, month by month from January 2018 to December 2025. For each county and measure, the map shows the trend: the typical change per year since 2018, estimated with Sen's slope and tested for significance using the Mann-Kendall test. For monthly data, each month is compared with the same month of 2018, controlling for seasonality. A county is outlined when its trend is found to be statistically significant.

Water tells an interesting - and unexpected - story in South Sudan. GRACE satellites show total water stored in the land rising in all 79 counties (significantly in 76, typically by 3.7 cm a year), and groundwater alone rose in every county too (significantly in 60). Yet the satellite rainfall records do not show a significant trend in any county. The unexpected increases in groundwater and surface water match trends in increased flooding coming down the White Nile after Lake Victoria surpassed its record-setting maximum level of water in 1964 during the wet season in 2020, 2021 and 2024.

In the 16 Sudd wetland counties, the average flooded area grew from about 2,800 km² in 2018 to 13,200 km² in 2022. Around Bentiu (Rubkona), land that was dry every March until 2020 has been about half under water each March since 2022. IOM recorded about 2.0 million flood displacements in 2020–2025, a third of them in the Sudd counties. In every hunger analysis since 2018, the Sudd counties had 12 to 21 percentage points more people in crisis than the rest of the country. In 2025, the year with the highest average water storage since 2018, the UN aid appeal was 46% funded and US government humanitarian funding fell to less than half of its 2024 level.
